Accountability that communities actually use

Most feedback mechanisms fail quietly. The ones that work are almost boring, and that is the point.

By Richard Osungu 4 min read · Accountability to affected populations

Most feedback mechanisms do not collapse. They fade. A suggestion box no one opens. A hotline that rings out. A complaints register that fills up and goes nowhere. On paper the mechanism exists; in practice it has quietly taught the community that speaking up is not worth the effort. Working on accountability to affected populations across humanitarian interventions, I have watched this failure mode more than any other.

The uncomfortable truth is that a broken feedback channel is worse than none, because it manufactures the appearance of listening while the substance leaks away. And the damage is not only to the relationship. It is to the data, because people who do not trust the channel stop telling you the truth through any of them.

Built for auditors, not for people

Feedback systems fail when they are designed to satisfy a donor requirement rather than to serve the person using them. The form is long, the language is technical, the channel is one an outsider chose. It ticks the compliance box and misses the human being entirely. The fix is not more sophistication. It is less friction: a channel people can actually reach, in a form they would actually use.

A channel people don't trust doesn't just stay silent. It teaches them silence.

Close the loop, visibly

The mechanisms that work share one unglamorous feature: the loop closes where people can see it. Someone raises an issue, gets a real acknowledgement, and, crucially, watches something change as a result. That visible movement is the entire mechanism. Without it, you have a mailbox; with it, you have a relationship in which feedback is worth giving because it demonstrably does something.

Trust compounds, and so does honesty

Once a community sees the loop close a few times, something shifts. Reports get more candid, problems surface earlier, and the quality of everything you monitor improves, because accountability and data quality turn out to be the same discipline viewed from two angles. The thing I would never build again is the elaborate, unanswerable channel. The thing I would build every time is the simple one that keeps its promises.
