Operationalizing a MEAL system across 528 community health units

Standing up monitoring for malaria at this scale is less about dashboards, and more about the people who enter the first number.

By Richard Osungu 6 min read · Global Fund Malaria (M&E)

The hardest problem in a monitoring system this size is not the analysis. It is trust in a single cell of a spreadsheet, the one a community health promoter fills in at a doorstep, hours before it ever becomes a rate, a trend, or a decision. Under the Global Fund–supported malaria programme (where Amref Health Africa is Principal Recipient and TINADA, my organisation, the Sub-Recipient), I coordinate the MEAL function across 528 community health units in Kakamega and Kisii counties, and almost everything I care about traces back to that first number.

This is monitoring and evaluation, not a research study: routine, high-frequency, and unforgiving of friction. The community case management of malaria (CCMm) data has to arrive every month, from every unit, clean enough to act on, and the system only works if the people at its edges can actually use it under real conditions.

The unit of truth is a person

Behind the 528 units sit coaches, peer educators and community health promoters, the people who test a febrile child, record the result, and start treatment. A dashboard is only as honest as they are diligent, and diligence is a design problem before it is a training one. If the register is confusing, the incentives misaligned, or the feedback invisible, quality decays no matter how elegant the analysis downstream.

So the first months were spent close to the ground: simplifying tools, aligning the indicators to what health workers already understood, and making sure disaggregation (by age, sex and sub-county) was something the form captured naturally rather than something we reconstructed later under pressure.

A dashboard is only as honest as the promoter who enters the first number.

Quality assurance as a routine, not an event

The instinct with data quality is to schedule an audit, a big, quarterly reckoning. But at this cadence, a quarterly catch is three months too late. What holds the data together is a set of small, repeated rituals: verification checks that run the moment figures are entered, a short monthly reconciliation between the community registers and the reporting system, and a habit of tracing any implausible jump back to the unit that produced it while the memory is still fresh.

None of this is glamorous. It is the difference, though, between a number a sub-county health team will stake a decision on and one they quietly work around.

From surveillance to a supervision shortlist

Monitoring earns its keep only when it changes what someone does on Monday. The point of the CCMm pipeline is not the trend line. It is the shortlist it produces: which units are seeing rising positivity, which have gone silent, and where a supervision visit will do the most good this month. Analysis turns 528 rows into a handful of names. And because it is a single reproducible notebook, the same code runs on either county's monthly export: Kakamega now, Kisii on the same pipeline, so the shortlist regenerates itself wherever the data comes from.

The reproducible notebook behind that shortlist is written up separately, with the code and the rest of the charts. If you want the mechanics (the data-quality checks, the population-adjusted incidence, the hotspot logic), that is the place to look.

What survives contact with the field

If there is a lesson I keep relearning, it is that a MEAL system is not the framework document. It is the set of habits it leaves behind in the people who run it. Get the tools, the rituals and the feedback loops right, and the indicators mostly take care of themselves. Get them wrong, and no amount of downstream cleverness will save the numbers. At 528 units, that margin is the whole job.
