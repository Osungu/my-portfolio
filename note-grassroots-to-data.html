From grassroots leadership to data analytics

Bridging community mobilization and MEAL frameworks, and why the two were never separate disciplines.

By Richard Osungu (Binsi Mwakolo) 6 min read · Personal essay

In the modern professional landscape, we often separate data science from human science. We treat data pipelines, statistical modeling, and Monitoring, Evaluation, Accountability and Learning (MEAL) frameworks as cold, sterile, backend processes. But my journey into the world of numbers taught me an entirely different truth: data is simply human behaviour captured in metrics.

Long before I began building professional data models, cleaning complex datasets, or designing quantitative research methodologies, my journey as an analytical strategist began in a highly dynamic, unpredictable environment: grassroots youth mobilization in western Kenya.

The foundation: organizing on the pitch

In late 2015, while serving as Vice Chairperson of the Nyakach University Students' Association (NUSA), we faced a major challenge. The long December holidays were approaching, and hundreds of university and secondary school students were returning home to rural villages with very little to keep them productively engaged.

The concern was wider than students. Idle youth across Nyakach were increasingly seen as a security risk, so we opened the door to every young person in the sub-county, not only those returning from university and secondary school.

Instead of deploying traditional, top-down academic seminars, we chose a universal language: sports. We designed and launched an extensive, multi-sport regional holiday tournament covering boys' and girls' football, netball and volleyball.

To the casual observer, it was just a festive sporting carnival. Behind the scenes it was an intensive exercise in logistics, resource allocation and project management. We had to map out tournament brackets across multiple wards, manage tight equipment budgets, coordinate village pitch locations, and handle stakeholder communications with local elders and administrators. More importantly, we used the massive captive audience at these games to run public youth advocacy, mentorship forums, and to pitch a bigger vision for our sub-county.

The tournament also drew scouts, and two of our footballers were signed into Kenyan junior leagues on the strength of what they played that December. Talent had always been there. What was missing was a stage where anyone could see it.

Data is simply human behaviour captured in metrics.

Stepping into the chairmanship: data in action

When I took over the mantle as NUSA Chairperson in 2016, my cabinet inherited a highly mobilized youth network, one I had gone well outside my comfort zone to build. We decided to test the limits of what a self-funded student body could achieve.

Within the span of a single month, our nine-member executive team organized and executed an aggressive academic mentorship tour across 15 different secondary schools in Nyakach. We did not rely on corporate funding or political handouts. We pooled our pocket money, mapped out a tight logistical routing matrix, and physically stood before thousands of students to provide career path guidance and university application insights.

It was during this era that the Nyakach Go Green Initiative was formally born. To solve local environmental degradation and soil erosion along our riparian zones, we did not just want a one-off tree-planting photo opportunity. We needed a sustainable, accountable system.

We launched a digital crowdfunding drive on the M-Changa platform. Because we were asking for financial backing from the Nyakach diaspora, university alumni and local professionals, transparency was non-negotiable. We established a rigorous, treasurer-controlled setup to track every single shilling. Those crowdsourced funds went directly toward purchasing seedlings, managing nursery logistics, and funding student-led school environmental clubs that would nurture the trees after we returned to our respective university campuses.

We planted across all 54 secondary schools then in Nyakach, along with a few selected primary schools. The initiative has run every year since its inception, which is the part I am proudest of. A campaign that outlives the cabinet that started it was never really about the cabinet.

Connecting the dots to MEAL and research

Years later, when people look up my name online, they primarily find my professional portfolio, focused on data analytics, quantitative research, and the intricacies of MEAL systems.

But when I design a data collection tool, audit an indicator tracking table, or evaluate the socio-economic impact of a corporate development program today, I am using the exact same muscles I developed as NUSA Chairman in 2016.

Managing an M-Changa fund for environmental sustainability taught me the core principles of financial accountability. Running a 15-school tour within 30 days taught me how to optimize logistical workflows. Organizing youth tournaments across multiple sports fields taught me how to monitor real-time project outputs.

Whether I am known in the data studio as Richard Osungu or across the regional sports networks by my old moniker, Binsi Mwakolo, the philosophy remains completely unchanged. Effective development requires both an authentic understanding of the people on the ground and an unyielding commitment to data-driven accountability.
