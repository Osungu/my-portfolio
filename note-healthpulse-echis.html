Integrating HealthPulse AI into eCHIS: early signals on diagnostic accuracy

When an AI reader sits between a health worker and a rapid test, accuracy is only half the story. Adoption is the other half.

By Richard Osungu 5 min read · Implementation research · TINADA × Medic Afya

Unlike the routine monitoring I run for the malaria programme, this is a research question, deliberately scoped, time-bound, and designed to feed a decision that reaches beyond any single county. It asks what happens when a HealthPulse AI reader is placed alongside the malaria rapid diagnostic test in the hands of a community health promoter, and how that integration behaves inside eCHIS, the national electronic community health information system.

The temptation is to reduce it to one figure: is the AI more accurate than the human eye reading a faint test line? That matters. But an evaluation that stops there answers the wrong question, because a diagnostic tool does not live in a lab. It lives at a doorstep, in a health worker's hands, in the seconds before a treatment decision.

Three things change at once

Introduce the reader and you change accuracy, confidence and workflow simultaneously. The device may catch a positive a tired eye would miss; it may also make a promoter second-guess a call they would otherwise have trusted, or add a step that costs a minute at every visit. Designing the study meant instrumenting all three, not just whether the diagnosis was right, but whether the health worker acted on it, and what it cost them to do so.

A tool that improves diagnosis but slows the health worker at the doorstep will not scale, no matter how good its sensitivity looks on paper.

Separating the signal from the novelty

Anything new gets used carefully at first. Health workers pay closer attention, supervisors hover, and performance lifts for reasons that have nothing to do with the technology. A credible evaluation has to hold that novelty effect at arm's length, comparing against a clear reference standard, watching how behaviour settles once the device stops being new, and resisting the urge to read the first enthusiastic weeks as the steady state.

Adoption is the other half

The most useful early signal is not a sensitivity statistic. It is whether promoters reach for the reader without being told to. Adoption is where good tools quietly fail: if the workflow does not fit the rhythm of a home visit, the device migrates to the bottom of the bag. So the study treats uptake, and the reasons behind it, as findings in their own right, gathered through the same eCHIS records the programme already runs on.

The question behind the question

Ultimately this feeds a national conversation about digital-health scale-up: not just whether AI-assisted diagnosis works, but whether it is worth what it costs to run at scale, and under what conditions. That is why accuracy, adoption and cost sit in the same study. The decision it informs is an investment decision, and investment decisions need all three.
