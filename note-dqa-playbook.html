A practical DQA playbook: how we cut data discrepancies by 30%

Quarterly audits sound bureaucratic, until you watch one catch a systematic error before it reaches a donor report.

By Richard Osungu 5 min read · Data governance

Data-quality assessment has an image problem. It sounds like paperwork, a box to tick before a report goes out. Managing M&E for the Aquaculture Business Development Programme, I ran quarterly DQAs that cut discrepancies by roughly 30%, and the reason they worked had nothing to do with paperwork. They worked because they were designed to change where errors come from, not just to find them after the fact.

Here is the playbook, stripped to its useful parts.

Sample where the risk is

You cannot re-check everything, so do not try. A good DQA is risk-based: it concentrates on the indicators that drive decisions, the sites with a history of trouble, and the figures that look implausible against their own past. A round-number spike, a total that does not reconcile with its parts, a site reporting suspiciously smooth results. These are where you spend your attention, not on a uniform sweep that treats every cell as equally likely to be wrong.

Trace it back to the source document

The single most valuable move in any audit is following a reported number back to the register or form it came from. Most discrepancies are not fraud; they are transcription, a misread column, a double-count at aggregation. Tracing a sample of figures to source tells you which, and more importantly tells you whether the problem is one careless entry or a systematic fault in how the data is collected everywhere.

The goal isn't to catch errors after the fact. It's to make the correct entry the easiest one to make.

Fix it at source, then close the loop fast

Finding an error is worth little if the same error returns next quarter. The value is in feeding the correction back quickly, to the person who made the entry, while they still remember the context, and where the fault is systematic, changing the form or the definition so the mistake becomes hard to make. A confusing field gets simplified; an ambiguous indicator gets a clearer rule. Aligning everything to the programme's KPIs kept those definitions consistent from the register all the way up to the national dashboard.

Why the number fell

The 30% reduction did not come from catching more mistakes. It came from designing them out, closing the loop fast enough, and at source often enough, that the common errors stopped recurring. A DQA is at its best when it quietly makes itself less necessary over time.
