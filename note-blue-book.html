From a value-chain study to the Blue Book

Evidence rarely walks itself into policy. It gets there when you design for the decision, not the document.

By Richard Osungu 6 min read · Value-chain study

Our aquaculture value-chain study could easily have ended its life as a PDF on a shared drive, thorough, well-referenced, and unread. Instead it became a Blue Book adopted by government for planning and resource allocation. The difference was not the rigour of the research. It was a set of choices, made early, about who the study was really for.

Commissioned under the Aquaculture Business Development Programme (ABDP), a Government of Kenya and IFAD initiative, the brief was to identify and document the stakeholders and players across Kenya's aquaculture sector. The easy version of that job produces a report. The useful version produces something a planner reaches for again and again.

Design for the decision, not the document

A study aimed at a decision looks different from one aimed at a deliverable. We worked backwards from the choices planners actually had to make: where to invest, which parts of the value chain were thin, who needed to be at the table, and let those questions shape what we collected and how we structured it. The report was not the goal; it was the by-product of answering questions someone was going to act on.

A report answers a question. A reference document answers the next ten.

Map people in the language they use

Stakeholder mapping only travels into policy if it is legible to the people in it. We described the value chain in the categories planners and industry already used, not in bespoke research jargon that would need translating. That single choice, meeting the audience in their own vocabulary, is what let the findings move from our hands into theirs without friction.

Make the data accessible, not just available

Available data sits in an annex. Accessible data is organised so a non-specialist can find what they need and trust it. Turning a fragmented sector into a single, navigable reference: consistent definitions, clear provenance, a structure that mirrored how decisions were made, is what earned it a place in the planning process rather than the archive.

Usable, not shelved

The lesson has stayed with me across every study since, including the malaria work I do now: evidence earns its influence by being usable at the moment of decision. Rigour gets you in the door. Whether the work is picked up or shelved is decided by choices about framing, language and accessibility, the unglamorous craft of making good evidence easy to act on.
